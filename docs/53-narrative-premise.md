# The Narrative Premise — the owner's one-pager, as received

**Status: an inbound premise, 2026-09-26 — NOT a ruling and NOT the story bible.** The owner drafted
this with an outside writing agent that had not read the game, and handed it over with the
instruction to *"take what they said with a grain of salt in terms of what's locked, what can be
changed… anything about monetization, the characters, etc."* and to use it *"as an overall possible
guide or premise to help get the game jump-started."* What the owner singled out as the part he
wants: *"Mara might be a single mom and doesn't have the ability to possibly have a child, and she
finds the flower to be somewhat of a daughter that she never had. Some real emotional hooks that fit
our audience."* The original `.docx` stays on the owner's machine; this is its text, verbatim, with
the design desk's read at the end. The story bible that turns this into chapters is a separate,
critic-tested document.

## The one-pager, verbatim

High-level story direction for handoff to narrative development.


### Premise

A woman who could not have a child raises a flower that believes she is its mother — while the rare-plant underworld closes in on the only living specimen of a species everyone thought was a myth.

Mara Vance, mid-thirties, leaves a marriage that ended badly and inherits her late grandmother's derelict botanical estate. The will attaches a condition: restore the garden within the year or it passes to her cousin. In the ruined greenhouse she finds a locked seed tin. She plants what is inside. It blooms, looks up at her, and asks if she is its mother.

Tone: warm soap opera with a mystery underneath. Gossip Harbor's interpersonal heat crossed with Merge Mansion's slow-drip secret, set in the genteel and quietly vicious world of competitive flower shows.

Audience: women 35–55, the core demographic for narrative-driven casual titles.


### The Wonder

The central flower — the Wonder — is Gran Ophelia's impossible hybrid, the specimen the flower-show underworld has hunted for forty years. The sprout that imprints on Mara is the only living one.

The FTUE beat. Player plants the seed. It blooms. It asks: "Are you my mommy?" Mara deflects — "...I'm Mara." Nothing is explained. This is also the game's primary UA creative.

Rules that govern it:

Wonder speaks only to Mara. Other characters hear a plant.

Wonder ages across bloom cycles: toddler-curious at first, wry and perceptive later.

Wonder holds memories that are not its own — a kitchen, a lullaby, a woman's hands. These are Gran's, and they are the mystery engine.

Wonder never asks for money. Not once, not in any form. Bram is the gacha voice, the Baroness is the paywall. Wonder is only ever the reason to come back. This is a hard rule, not a preference — a maternal hook attached to a purchase prompt is the screenshot that defines the game.

Canon name is "Wonder" for all marketing; an optional in-game nickname is fine.


### Mara's emotional core

Mara wanted a child and it did not happen. That is the wound the FTUE lands on, and it is why the flower's question works instead of merely being cute. It is never stated up front and never used as a monetization lever.

The thread is gated on Fair reputation and bloom cycles — intimacy with people, and time spent with Wonder. The player earns it by being kind and by staying, not by winning or paying.

A five-beat drip across the full game:

Cycle 1 — Wonder calls her mommy. Mara deflects and changes the subject. Nothing explained.

Fair 3 — Delphine asks about kids. Mara's answer is clean, practiced, and obviously used before.

Cycle 3 — Mara catches herself humming a lullaby to the sprout and stops mid-bar.

Fair 6 + Cycle 5 — Theo finds a half-painted nursery in the east wing. Neither of them says anything.

Act 4 — She says it out loud, once. Then never again.

Restraint rules: beat 5 is four lines of dialogue maximum. Mara never explains herself, never monologues about it, and no other character offers consolation on the subject. The restraint is what makes it land, and it is also what keeps it from reading as exploitative.

### Story arc

Five acts. Acts are gated by total reputation, not by calendar or seasons.

Act 1 — The Inheritance. Mara arrives at the ruined estate with a one-year restore-or-forfeit clause hanging over her. She finds the locked seed tin in the greenhouse, plants it, and it speaks. Delphine adopts her. Theo is already there and says very little.

Act 2 — The Circuit. Money is the problem, so Mara enters the local flower shows. Wonder wins everything, which is exactly the wrong kind of attention. Bram appears. So does Julian, who needs the estate sold and is running out of time.

Act 3 — The Memories. Wonder starts remembering things it could not possibly know. Memories unlock through collection completion — every restored plot returns one petal of Gran's past. The Society begins courting Mara seriously; the Baroness wants the specimen, not the friendship.

Act 4 — The Daughter. The reveal: Gran had a daughter the family never mentions, given away decades ago. Gran spent thirty years breeding a flower to hold what was left of her. It partly worked. Wonder is not a plant that talks — it is a plant that remembers being loved. Mara's own confession lands here, in four lines.

Act 5 — The Wonder Cup. Julian and the cartel force the choice: sell Wonder and save the estate, or protect it and lose everything. Mara refuses. The circuit is exposed.

Post-climax hook. One of Wonder's memories is recent. The daughter is alive, and the flower knows where she is. This is what keeps live-ops running past the emotional peak instead of writing into a finale.

Recommendation on the daughter reveal: given away, not died young. Grief without a dead child, a cleaner reunion, and it buys an entire second volume.


### How story is delivered

Two separate axes. Keeping them separate is load-bearing.

Reputation — permanent, never resets, gates all story. This is the progression spine. No seasons, no turn timer, no calendar pressure.

Bloom cycles — the prestige reset, triggered by the player when Wonder goes to seed. Carries no story weight of its own beyond Wonder aging. If a reset ever wiped reputation, the narrative would unravel and players would stop cycling.

Factional reputation. Three independent permanent tracks:

Every meaningful bloom has a factional destination. One specimen, three sinks: enter it at the Fair, sell it to the Society, or trade it to Bram. Same flower, three different games.

Four beat triggers, all four in use:

Threshold — Society 4 fires "The Baroness Extends an Invitation." Predictable and schedulable.

Composite — total reputation across all three gates the main spine. Never hard-block the main story behind one faction or single-path players get stranded.

Divergence — Society exceeding Fair by 20 fires "Delphine Doesn't Wave Back." The best writing in the system, because the player caused it without being asked to choose.

Neglect — a faction untouched for 14 days sends a nudge beat. Cheap re-engagement with story attached.

Always allow repair. A make-amends questline that costs resources and restores standing. Permanently locking free players out of a third of the content is a churn machine.

Content multiplier: because chapters are faction-colored, one scripted scene can ship in three variants with different characters present — roughly 1.6× the content for 1.3× the writing.


### Guardrails

Non-negotiables for whoever writes this.

Wonder never appears in a purchase prompt. The maternal hook and the monetization voice stay in separate characters, permanently.

Mara never explains her childlessness. Imply it. One spoken confession in the whole script, four lines, then silence.

No gender select. Fixed female lead. The flower's question is the hook; the branch was never the hook, and branching doubles art, romance states, VO and localization while splitting UA creative.

Front-load mystery fragments. Under 5% of players reach Act 4. Wonder should say something in the first session that does not make sense until much later. Pay off the mystery in slivers from hour one.

Cap story beats per day regardless of reputation gain. Otherwise the highest-spending players finish six months of writing in a weekend and churn hardest.

Sympathetic antagonist. Julian is desperate, not villainous. Cartoon villains underperform on retention in this genre.

Two risks worth flagging to production:

Cute art plus gacha draws attention from ad platforms and EU/UK loot-box regulators. Review during pre-production, not at cert.

A child-coded character adjacent to near-miss mechanics is a press risk. The Wonder rule above is the mitigation and it has to be enforced in review, not trusted to good intentions.

## The desk's read — what fits the game as built, and what collides

Read this before the story bible is briefed. Nothing here is ruled; each line is priced for the
owner.

**Keep, and build the game around it:**

- **The hook.** A woman who could not have a child raises a flower that thinks she is its mother.
  It is Lily's Garden's register (grief, an inheritance, a garden) with a sharper twist, and that
  register is the proven one for women 35–55 — the audience Gossip Harbor, Merge Mansion, June's
  Journey and Lily's Garden all monetize. The restraint rules (one confession, four lines, never
  consoled) are what keep it from reading as exploitative, and they are rare in a first draft.
- **The flower never sells.** This is already the house rule — sacred moments stay clean, doc 37's
  ad and store placements never wear the flower's face. The one-pager makes it a story rule too.
- **Front-loaded mystery slivers.** The flower says something in session one that pays off much
  later. Cheap, and it is the whole retention argument for a story in an idle game.
- **The sympathetic antagonist; the alive-daughter hook for a second volume.**
- **"Acts gated by total reputation, never by calendar."** This is exactly the two-riders rule
  (doc 52): reputation is permanent, the story rides it, the Turn never touches it.

**Collides with the game as built — each priced:**

1. **"No seasons, no turn timer."** The game is the Garden Year: four seasons at four speeds, and
   the Turn. The premise's own inheritance clause — *restore the garden within the year or it
   passes to the cousin* — is a gift here: **the first Year is the will's deadline, and the first
   Turn is the moment Mara keeps the garden.** That gives the Turn a story meaning it has never had.
   Cost: none; it is the same structure with a reason.
2. **"Wonder" as the flower's name** collides with the Wonder Effect (the game's jackpot moment) and
   with the title. The flower needs its own name — the owner's call. Holly, Winter's hero flower,
   already exists with a voice; the bible should decide whether she is the same plant in winter, a
   sibling from the same seed tin, or a separate character.
3. **Mara as a fixed, named lead.** Today the flower talks to *you* — the player is the gardener,
   unnamed. The lane uses named female leads (Lily, Quinn), so a named lead is right for the market;
   the cost is that every existing flower line ("you") is re-read as Mara, and story dialogue needs a
   surface (the moments dialog exists; a two-portrait exchange does not). Priced as a writing pass
   over `FLOWER_LINES` plus one new dialog shape.
4. **Three factions with three reputation tracks.** Ruled otherwise: one reputation number, one
   trunk (doc 52). Factions survive as **people at the Stand** — Delphine, the Baroness, Bram are
   villagers who place orders and whose beats fire on chapters. Divergence beats ("Delphine doesn't
   wave back") can return later as flavour keyed to whose orders you filled, never as a second track.
5. **Bram as "the gacha voice", the Baroness as "the paywall".** Loot boxes are a priced default in
   this repo, not a decision (the 2026-09-21 entry), and a paywall character is a store decision that
   does not exist yet. The bible writes these characters as people; the store decides later what
   they sell, if anything. Note the coincidence: the game already has **Bram the Baker** as a
   customer in `data.js`; reuse or rename, the owner's call.
6. **"Underworld", "cartel", a bought judge.** The lane does soap, not crime (Gossip Harbor's
   secrets are affairs and inheritances). Keep the pressure interpersonal — Julian's debt, the
   Baroness's want, the show's snobbery — and let "the circuit" be genteel and vicious rather than
   criminal. Cost of ignoring this: tone drift that the cosy audience reads as a different game.
7. **40–60k words for year one.** Two people cannot write, localise and maintain that, and doc 27's
   finding stands: ~150 lines of writing is the cheapest differentiator this game has. v1 is eight
   chapters at a few hundred words each, plus villagers' order lines, plus the memory slivers.
   Acts 4 and 5 are a second volume. The alive-daughter hook is written *into* volume one's last
   sliver so live-ops has somewhere to go.
8. **"Cap story beats per day."** Already the shape of the reveal machinery (doc 47's moments dialog
   is one-at-a-time); with reputation rebalanced to the flowers' pace (2026-09-26), the cap is mostly
   the curve. Keep a hard daily cap anyway; it is one knob.
9. **Every chapter must change something visible** (the 2026-09-22 ruling). The premise's acts are
   dialogue-shaped; the bible maps each to a room we have — the greenhouse is the garden's backdrop
   restored, Delphine arrives at the Stand, Theo keeps the meadow, the Hollow wakes, Holly keeps
   Winter, the show is Fall's bed and its windfall. The visible change is the expensive half, and it
   is the part a builder can build.

**What the desk would test before believing any of it:** reviews of Lily's Garden, Gossip Harbor
and June's Journey for what players *praise* in the story (the beats, not the premise) and what
they complain about (pacing gates, paywalled chapters, drama that turns mean); and one question on
the flower's question — *"Are you my mommy?"* — is it the UA creative that cuts through, or the line
a store reviewer screenshots? The bible's market critic answers both.
